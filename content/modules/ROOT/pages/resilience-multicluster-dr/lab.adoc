An entire primary site is scaled to zero mid-flight; the mesh ejects it, retries onto the secondary, and the client — which never changes its address — keeps reading `HTTP 200`.

=== Brief

image::resilience-multicluster-dr/resilience-multicluster-dr-01-resilience-ladder.svg["The resilience ladder as two rows of rungs. The lower row is in-site resilience: a pod crash absorbed by Deployment replicas and probes, a node failure or drain absorbed by a PodDisruptionBudget with spread constraints, anti-affinity and autoscaler headroom, then a zone loss. The upper row is beyond one site: a whole site lost, absorbed by cross-site failover over the service mesh and Red Hat Service Interconnect, then a region lost, absorbed by OADP and GitOps re-materialization, and finally the service surviving."]

*The resilience ladder*

Every resilience mechanism absorbs one specific failure. Replicas and probes absorb a crashing pod. A PodDisruptionBudget, spread constraints and an autoscaler carry a service through a node drain or the loss of a zone. Those rungs are in-site resilience, and most teams already own them.

A whole site going dark — a data center, a cluster, in this demo a namespace — is a different class of problem. No number of replicas *inside* that site helps, because the site is the thing that is gone. Surviving it means failing over to another site automatically, with no client reconfigured and no request dropped.

*Cross-site failover with Service Mesh*

OpenShift Service Mesh puts an ingress gateway in front of both sites as one stable endpoint, and three small traffic rules do the rest. A `ServiceEntry` declares the two sites and tags each with its locality. A `DestinationRule` turns on locality-aware load balancing, so the primary is preferred, and outlier detection, so a site whose requests start failing is ejected from the pool. A `VirtualService` routes the stable endpoint through that host with retries that are allowed to move to the other site.

Nothing about the client changes. It calls the same address before, during and after the failure — and the requests that would have been dropped at the moment of failure are the ones the retries carry across to the second site.

*In this demo*

. A claims service running resiliently in a primary site, and a client logging which site answers it
. The entire primary site scaled to zero, and the client flipping to the secondary with every response still `HTTP 200`
. The primary restored, and traffic returning to it with nothing changed on the client

=== 1 · A resilient primary, and a client watching it — 3 min

Parasol's claims service runs in two sites, A and B, and a client calls it through one stable address once a second, logging which site answered. It reads site A, the primary — and site A is itself resilient: three replicas, a disruption budget, spread across nodes, an autoscaler holding the floor.

image::resilience-multicluster-dr/resilience-multicluster-dr-02-resilient-site.png["The OpenShift console Deployments list for the site-a project, with one row for parasol-claims showing a status of 3 of 3 pods and the labels app=parasol-claims and site=A."]

*Why it matters*

This is the resilience most teams already have, and it is genuinely good: a pod crash or a node drain is a non-event here. It is also the prerequisite for what comes next rather than a substitute for it — every one of these replicas lives in the site that is about to fail.

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
CLIENT={user}-client
SITEA={user}-site-a
SITEB={user}-site-b
oc get deploy,pdb,hpa -n $SITEA
oc logs deploy/claims-client -n $CLIENT --tail=5
----

*What just happened*

Three of three replicas, a budget that keeps two of them available through any voluntary disruption, and an autoscaler holding the floor at three. Every one of the client's last five lines reads `HTTP 200  served-by-site=A`.

=== 2 · The whole primary site fails — 5 min

The disaster is the bluntest one available: site A is scaled to zero. There is no primary site any more — no replicas to reschedule, nothing left to retry against locally. The client is not told, and its address does not change.

image::resilience-multicluster-dr/resilience-multicluster-dr-01-failover-log-flip.png["A terminal tailing the claims-client log across a site failure: nine consecutive lines read HTTP 200 served-by-site=A, then every following line reads HTTP 200 served-by-site=B, with the oc scale --replicas=0 command visible above."]

*Why it matters*

"We have a second data center" and "we fail over to it automatically" are different capabilities, and only one of them survives a bad night. This is the game-day drill that tells them apart — and the routing that produces it is three small resources and no application code.

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
oc scale deploy/parasol-claims -n {user}-site-a --replicas=0
----

*On screen* — the tailing client log flips from `served-by-site=A` to `served-by-site=B` within a few seconds, and every line on both sides of the flip is still `HTTP 200`. A single request through the same stable endpoint, answered by whichever site is live, makes the same point on demand:

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
oc exec deploy/claims-client -n {user}-client -- curl -s -m3 http://claims-stable/
----

*What just happened*

The primary site is gone and the client did not drop a request. Outlier detection noticed site A failing and ejected it from the pool, so new requests went to site B; the retries caught the handful of in-flight requests that raced the ejection and landed them on site B too. About a second, end to end, on the cluster this was measured on.

=== 3 · Recovery, with nothing changed on the client — 3 min

Site A comes back with three replicas. Once the fresh pods are Ready and the mesh's ejection of site A lapses — roughly thirty to ninety seconds — the mesh trusts the primary again and traffic returns to it.

image::resilience-multicluster-dr/resilience-multicluster-dr-02-mesh-failover.svg["The failover path: an external client calling one stable endpoint, a mesh ingress gateway pinned to site A's locality, a bold arrow to site A's three claims replicas as the normal locality-preferred path, and a dashed arrow to site B's replicas labeled: on A failure, outlier detection ejects A and retries flip to B."]

*Why it matters*

Failover that cannot fail back is a one-way door, and a door nobody wants to open. Here both directions are the same mechanism — locality prefers the primary, outlier detection decides whether it is trustworthy — so returning to normal is not a second procedure to write, test and remember.

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
oc scale deploy/parasol-claims -n {user}-site-a --replicas=3
# 30-90s later, once the fresh pods are Ready, the client log returns to served-by-site=A
----

*What just happened*

Failover and failback, both automatic, both invisible to the client. If site A were a real data center and site B a real second cluster, this is how losing one would look — and Red Hat Service Interconnect is how the two sites would be connected in the first place, over a mutual-TLS overlay rather than a VPN.

== Frequently asked questions

*The whole primary site went to zero — how did the client not drop a single request?*::
Two mesh features cooperate. Outlier detection on the `DestinationRule` notices site A's requests failing and ejects it from the load-balancing pool, so new requests go to site B. Retries on the `VirtualService`, which are allowed to move to the other site, catch the handful of in-flight requests that raced the ejection: a request that lands on the just-failed site is retried and answered by site B. Without the retries there would be a brief burst of errors at the moment of failure; with them, the failover drops nothing.

*Why does traffic prefer site A instead of splitting evenly across both sites?*::
Because the ingress gateway is pinned to site A's locality and the `DestinationRule` turns on locality-aware load balancing. Istio sends traffic to the nearest locality first — site A at priority 0, site B as the failover at priority 1 — which is primary/backup by design. Remove the locality pin and the same three resources round-robin both sites instead.

*Is this a real multi-cluster failover, or a simulation?*::
The topology is simulated and the plumbing is real. Three namespaces on one cluster stand in for the client and the two sites. The `ServiceEntry`, `DestinationRule` and `VirtualService`, the locality-aware routing, the outlier detection and the retries are identical across two real clusters in two regions: the endpoints would point at the other cluster's services, reached over a Service Interconnect network, and the gateway would be pinned to the local region.

*Several teams share this mesh and want the same stable hostname — don't they collide?*::
They would, which is why all three resources set `+exportTo: ["."]+` to scope them to their own namespace. Istio's default is to publish a traffic resource mesh-wide, so without that field one merged view of the hostname is assembled from every team's endpoints and a gateway can be programmed with someone else's sites — returning `200` the whole time, which is what makes it hard to spot. A single global service name is the pattern; the scope, not the name, is what keeps teams apart.

*Where do backup and restore fit alongside this?*::
Underneath it, as the platform's safety net for the catastrophes failover cannot cover — destroyed data, a lost cluster or region. OpenShift API for Data Protection backs up and restores an application's Kubernetes objects and its persistent-volume data, and a GitOps-managed platform re-materializes itself on a fresh cluster from Git. The division of labor is the useful part: the platform has your back for disaster recovery, and designing the service to be resilient and to fail over is the application team's job — which is what this demo shows.

In *Terminal 2* (the lower terminal), start a request loop: two calls a second for three minutes, counting served and failed, naming the pod that answered:

[source,sh,role="execute send-to-tty-bottom",subs="attributes+"]
----
oc exec deploy/claims-client -n {user}-client -- sh -c 'ok=0; failed=0; end=$(( $(date +%s) + 180 ))
while [ $(date +%s) -lt $end ]; do
  if r=$(curl -sf -m2 http://claims.{user}-site-a.svc:8080/); then ok=$((ok+1)); else failed=$((failed+1)); fi
  echo "$(date -u +%T)  ok=$ok  failed=$failed  $(echo "$r" | grep -o "parasol-claims-[a-z0-9]*-[a-z0-9]*")"
  sleep 0.5
done
echo "DONE: $ok served, $failed failed"'
----

In *Terminal 1*, evict two claims pods back to back; the `evict` helper posts the same `Eviction` request as `oc adm drain`.

*Predict, then run:* the budget has room for *one* disruption. What happens to the second eviction?

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
oc wait pdb/parasol-claims -n $SITEA --for=jsonpath='{.status.disruptionsAllowed}'=1 --timeout=90s
evict() { echo '{"apiVersion":"policy/v1","kind":"Eviction","metadata":{"name":"'"$1"'"}}' | oc create --raw /api/v1/namespaces/$SITEA/pods/$1/eviction -f - ; echo; }
P1=$(oc get pod -n $SITEA -l app=parasol-claims -o jsonpath='{.items[0].metadata.name}')
P2=$(oc get pod -n $SITEA -l app=parasol-claims -o jsonpath='{.items[1].metadata.name}')
evict $P1
evict $P2
oc get pdb parasol-claims -n $SITEA
----

[%collapsible]

.Why `exportTo: ["."]` is load-bearing
[%collapsible]
====

The same discipline applies in production: on a shared mesh, export only what other namespaces genuinely need to reach.

.Why the brief pause, and why no `404`/`503`
[%collapsible]
====
Between the last `A` and the first `B` you may see a *one- or two-second gap* in the log (the failover request took a retry hop, so the once-a-second loop skipped a beat) — but *no error line*, however long site A stays down. Two settings make that true:

* Outlier detection does not eject site A for good. Each ejection lasts `baseEjectionTime` times the number of ejections so far (30 s, then 60 s, then 90 s), and when one lapses the gateway sends a single request to site A to see whether it is back. During the outage that request fails.
* `retryRemoteLocalities: true` lets the retry of that request move to site B. Without it, Envoy retries only inside site A's locality, every attempt hits the dead site, and the client logs one `HTTP 503  served-by-site=none` each time an ejection lapses: about 30 s into the outage, and again about 100 s in.

The same timer sets the failback. Traffic returns to site A when its current ejection lapses after the new pods are Ready, so failback takes tens of seconds and grows with the length of the outage.
====

== Challenge (optional)
⏱ *no hand-holding*

You have the mechanics. Try these on your own:

* *Fail over the RHSI VAN too.* Add a *second* Connector in DC-A on the *same* routing key, so the Listener has two backends across the VAN; fail one and reason about how RHSI load-balances and fails over across sites, the cross-*cluster* analog of exercise 2.
* *Break the token redemption on purpose.* Create the `AccessToken` with a *truncated* `ca`, watch the `Link` never reach `Ready`, then fix it: the single most common RHSI support case.
* *Tune the failover window.* Set `outlierDetection.baseEjectionTime` to `5s`, then `120s`; fail site A each time and time the failback. Then delete `retryRemoteLocalities: true`, keep site A down a full minute, and find the `503` when the ejection lapses.
* *Header-pin a site.* Add a `VirtualService` `match` on a header (say `x-parasol-site: b`) that routes *explicitly* to site B regardless of health, on top of the automatic failover: the "send my test traffic to the DR site" pattern.

When you are done, run `ws verify {module-id}` in the Terminal, or `ws prep {module-id} --yes` to return to the entry state (resilient sites and client up, failover routing and RHSI objects removed).
