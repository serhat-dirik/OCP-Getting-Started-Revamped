Two guardrails that work in opposite directions.

The first keeps a value *out* of a place it must never be. The claims database password exists in exactly one system that is designed to hold it, and the Kubernetes Secret your application reads is derived from that system rather than maintained alongside it. You proved the derivation is live by rotating the value and watching the Secret follow.

The second keeps a *shape* out of a cluster that should not accept it. You read a policy that was already governing your own actions, broke each of its rules on purpose, and were refused each time with the policy's own words. Then you wrote one yourself and had the API server compile and judge it without creating anything.

== Map to your org

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| If your organisation… | Then the piece that matters here is…

| Keeps credentials in a vault the platform team owns, and applications never see it
| The `SecretStore` boundary. It is namespaced on purpose: one team's store is not another team's, and that is an authorization decision expressed as an object rather than as a convention.

| Has secrets in Git today and knows it
| Rotation, not storage. Moving the value is the easy half; the test of whether you have finished is whether changing it is a write or a project.

| Runs admission webhooks already
| `ValidatingAdmissionPolicy` removes a running component from the critical path. A webhook that is down is an outage or an open door, depending on its `failurePolicy`. An in-process CEL expression cannot be down.

| Is trying to give developers more self-service without more risk
| The four expressions you read. Self-service is not "fewer rules" — it is rules exact enough that the safe action is also the easy one.
|===

== When *not* to do it this way

*Do not reach for admission policy to enforce something RBAC already expresses.* If the rule is "this group may not create this kind of object at all", that is a `Role`, and it is simpler, faster and more obvious to a reader. Admission earns its place only when the answer depends on what is *inside* the object.

*Do not use `failurePolicy: Fail` on a policy whose match is broad, until you have watched it in `Ignore` for a while.* A policy that is wrong and failing closed refuses real work across the whole cluster, and the people it refuses will not know why.

*Do not treat an external secret store as an audit boundary on its own.* The value lands in a Kubernetes Secret, and anyone who can read Secrets in that namespace can read it. What you have moved is the value's *distribution*, not its final resting place — which is why the namespace's RBAC still matters just as much as it did.

*Do not mount a rotating secret as an environment variable and expect rotation to reach the process.* Environment is read once at container start. If the rotation must take effect without a restart, mount it as a volume and re-read the file.

== Go deeper

* `ValidatingAdmissionPolicy` supports `auditAnnotations` and a `Warn` action as well as denial — a policy can record or nag before it ever refuses, which is how a rule is safely introduced to a busy cluster.
* `ValidatingAdmissionPolicyBinding` is what scopes a policy to namespaces, and one policy can be bound several times with different parameters. The split between policy and binding is what makes a rule reusable rather than copy-pasted.
* The External Secrets Operator supports `PushSecret` for the other direction, and `ClusterSecretStore` when a store genuinely is cluster-wide. Both are the same idea with the arrow or the scope reversed.
