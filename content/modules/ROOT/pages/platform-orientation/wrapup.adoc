== What you built

Starting from an empty project, you deployed Parasol's claims portal from a container image, scaled it to three replicas, watched the platform replace a Pod you deliberately killed, published it at a public URL with a Route, and read its logs and events — first in the console, then in six `oc` commands.
You also asked OpenShift Lightspeed an operational question without leaving the console — or, where Lightspeed wasn't available on your cluster, found the same answer with `oc get events` and `oc logs`.

== Map to your org

Take these back to how your teams run software today:

. *Who runs your clusters, and what do you self-serve versus file a ticket for?*
On OpenShift, deploying, scaling, and exposing an app were all self-service in your own project.
Which of those are ticket-and-wait in your organization right now — and what would change if they were not?
. *What dies when a VM dies today?*
You killed a Pod and the platform replaced it in seconds with no page.
Pick one real production service: if the box it runs on vanished tonight, how long until it is back, and who has to be awake for it?
. *Where are your pets?*
Name three "servers with names" your organization still hand-nurses.
For each, is it a pet because the *workload* genuinely needs to be (stateful, licensed, legacy) — or only because nobody has made it cattle yet?

== When *not* to do it this way

Self-healing, disposable workloads are the right default — but "the right default" is not "always".
Be honest about the edges:

* *Don't containerize a pet and then keep treating it like a pet.*
This one is about *containers*, not about the platform.
If you put a workload in a Pod but still SSH in to hand-edit it, restart it by hand, or keep its only copy of important data inside the container, you have taken on everything a container asks of you and gained none of what it gives back.
You have two good answers and they are both here: make it cattle — externalize its state and config, which the next modules walk you through — or run it as a *virtual machine on this same cluster*, which is a first-class choice rather than a consolation prize (the next point).
The only bad answer is the middle one: a container operated like a hand-fed server.
* *A VM is still the right answer sometimes.*
Not every workload should be a container: a legacy appliance, a kernel-module-heavy application, or a vendor-supported VM image may be genuinely better run as a virtual machine.
OpenShift can run those too — as first-class VMs beside your containers, through OpenShift Virtualization.
The skill is choosing, not forcing everything into a Pod.
* *Stateful workloads need the right primitive — not a different platform.*
Databases run in production on OpenShift every day, and most modern ones ship an Operator that handles replication, failover, backup and version upgrades for you.
Running your database in containers is a normal, well-trodden choice, not a risky one.
What it needs is the right building blocks rather than a bare Deployment: a StatefulSet or an Operator, so each replica keeps a stable identity and rolls out in order, and a PersistentVolumeClaim, so the data outlives any individual Pod.
Your claims portal today is stateless, which is why killing its Pod costs you nothing; a database is not, and swapping its Pod without those pieces in place is how people lose data.
That is exactly why storage gets a module of its own (*Storage & Stateful Apps*).
The lesson is *use the right primitive*, not *keep your data off the platform*.
* *Scale-to-zero and request-driven compute are their own tools.*
You scaled by hand today, and a Deployment will hold whatever replica count you give it — including through the hours nobody is using it.
Two other shapes exist when idle capacity is what you are paying for.
*Serverless* (Knative) runs a workload only while requests are arriving and takes it to zero in between, so a rarely-used service costs nothing while it waits (*Serverless Zero-to-Hero*).
*KEDA (Custom Metrics Autoscaler)* scales on an external signal such as queue depth rather than CPU, and it too can go to zero — right for work that only exists when something has queued it (*Observability, Health & Scale*).
"Three replicas, always" is a choice you are making, not the only shape on offer.
