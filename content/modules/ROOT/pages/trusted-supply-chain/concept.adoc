[.lead]
Parasol just failed a supplier security audit: nobody could prove which build produced the claims image running in production, or what was inside it.

The pipeline from the previous work builds and deploys `parasol-claims` reliably — but "it builds" is not the same as "it is trustworthy."
The auditor asked three questions Parasol could not answer: _what dependencies are in this image, who built it, and can you prove the running image is the one your pipeline produced?_
In this module you run one pipeline that answers those questions — a *software bill of materials* (what is inside), *image signing with SLSA provenance* (who built it, and proof the running image is intact), a *policy check* before deploy, and *signature admission* — so the cluster will *refuse to run* anything the pipeline did not build.

[NOTE]
.What "[ADS]" means, and what is actually included
====
This module uses *Red Hat Trusted Artifact Signer* (RHTAS: the keyless signing service and its admission policy controller) and *Conforma*, part of the *Red Hat Advanced Developer Suite* (RHADS), a separate subscription from a base OpenShift subscription, and *Red Hat Advanced Cluster Security for Kubernetes* (RHACS), included with OpenShift Platform Plus.
*Tekton Chains* ships with OpenShift Pipelines, and OpenShift has its own node-level signature policy (`ClusterImagePolicy` in `config.openshift.io`), so signing and verifying with a key are included the moment you have Pipelines.
====

[NOTE]
.Two halves of DevSecOps — scanning and trust
====
*Scanning* your code and images for known flaws — SAST (static analysis), SCA (dependency CVEs), DAST (the running app), configuration, and image risk — is its own module, *Application Security Testing* (a separate module).
*This* module is the other half: proving *provenance* and *signing and verifying* what you ship, so only *trusted* artifacts run.
Put plainly: that module asks _"is it free of known flaws?"_; this one asks _"is it provably ours?"_ — and a mature pipeline does *both*.
The pipeline here includes a scan step only as *context* — the place trust controls attach — so the scan _technique_ stays the other module's subject.
====

== What you'll learn

* The *supply-chain threat model* in plain words — "you ship your dependencies" — and what the *SLSA* (Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts) levels actually ask of your build _process_.
* The *signature / attestation / SBOM* (software bill of materials) *triangle*: three different facts about an image, and why you need all three.
* How *Tekton Chains* signs every pipeline-built image and emits *SLSA provenance* — outside the pipeline, with no step holding a key — and how you verify it yourself with `cosign`.
* *Keyless* signing: a ten-minute certificate for a *workload identity* instead of a stored key, and a *transparency log* (Rekor, in *Red Hat Trusted Artifact Signer*) that turns a signature into a public, tamper-evident record anyone can audit.
* The difference between a *build-time gate* (Conforma fails the release) and *run-time admission* (the policy controller refuses to run) — so a signature is *checked*, not merely produced.
* Where trust ends and *scanning* begins: catching flaws with SAST, SCA, DAST, and image risk is a separate module (*Application Security Testing*); here you meet scanning as a single decisive gate and spend your time proving *provenance* and *signing and verifying* what you ship.

== You ship your dependencies

The single idea under this whole module: *the code your team wrote is a small fraction of what you deploy.*
The `parasol-claims` image is mostly other people's code — the Quarkus runtime, its transitive libraries, the base operating-system layers — pulled in at build time.
That is normal and good; it is also the attack surface.
When a critical flaw is found in a widely used library (Log4Shell in `log4j-core`, in December 2021, is the canonical example), the hard questions are not about the code you wrote — they are *whether you can prove what is in each image, who built it, and that the one running in production is the one your pipeline shipped.*

A trusted software supply chain answers three questions about every artifact you build:

Provenance:: _Where did this come from?_ Who built it, from which source, with which builder — and can you prove the running image is that exact build, not a substitute.
Integrity:: _Has it been tampered with?_ A cryptographic *signature* that the image you run is byte-for-byte the image your pipeline produced.
Contents:: _What is inside?_ A *software bill of materials* (SBOM) — the itemized list of every component and version, produced at build time and _attestable_ so it travels with the signed image. (Turning that list into a vulnerability answer — "are we affected by the flaw that just broke the internet?" — is *scanning*, the subject of the Application Security Testing module; here the SBOM is the trust artifact that records _what shipped_.)

[TIP]
.SLSA, in one paragraph
====
*SLSA* (Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts, pronounced "salsa") is an industry framework that grades _how trustworthy your build process is_ — not your code, your *process*.
The levels climb roughly like this: *build on a hosted system* (not a laptop), *produce signed provenance* (the build system attests what it did), and *make that provenance tamper-resistant and complete*.
You do not need to memorize the level numbers.
What matters is the shape: a pipeline that builds on the cluster, signs what it produces, and attaches machine-readable provenance is *already* climbing SLSA — and that is exactly what Tekton Chains gives you in this module for free.
====

== The signature / attestation / SBOM triangle

Three artifacts travel with a trustworthy image, and they answer three *different* questions.
People conflate them constantly, so pin the distinction now:

[cols="1,3,2",options="header"]
|===
| Artifact | Answers | Produced by (in this lab)

| *Signature* | "This image is intact and came from us." A cryptographic signature over the image digest. | Tekton Chains, keyless, as its workload identity
| *Attestation (provenance)* | "Here is _how_ this image was built" — the pipeline and task, the builder, the parameters, the results, the timestamps. A signed *SLSA provenance* statement. | Tekton Chains (in-toto / SLSA)
| *SBOM* | "Here is _what is inside_" — every component and version. | The pipeline's `sbom` step (CycloneDX); its sha256 is in the signed provenance
|===

A signature with no SBOM tells you the image is authentic but not what is in it.
An SBOM with no signature tells you what _a_ build contained but not that _this_ image is that build.
You need all three, and they reinforce each other: the attestation is _signed_, it pins the SBOM by its hash, and the signature is what an admission gate _checks_.

== How the signing works

One signer: *Tekton Chains*, the build platform.
It signs every image a pipeline builds, and the SLSA provenance that says how it was built, *keyless* through *Red Hat Trusted Artifact Signer* (RHTAS, the Red Hat build of the sigstore project).
Conforma checks that signature and provenance before the pipeline deploys; the policy controller checks the same signature before every pod starts.
Every component runs in this cluster.

|===
| Component | Runs in | Role

| *Tekton Chains*
| `openshift-pipelines`
| Watches every TaskRun. When a build task reports the image it pushed, Chains signs the image and attaches the SLSA provenance (what was built, by which pipeline run and task, with which parameters and results), and stores both next to the image (`.sig`, `.att`). It is the build platform, not a pipeline step: no task holds a signing key or can sign for it.

| *SPIRE* (Zero Trust Workload Identity Manager)
| `zero-trust-workload-identity-manager`
| Gives the Chains controller a short-lived token naming its *SPIFFE* (Secure Production Identity Framework for Everyone) ID, `spiffe://<trust domain>/ns/openshift-pipelines/sa/tekton-chains-controller`, after checking on the node which namespace and service account it really runs as.

| *Fulcio*
| `trusted-artifact-signer`
| Certificate authority. Checks the token against the keys SPIRE publishes, then issues a certificate valid for 10 minutes with that SPIFFE ID in it.

| *Rekor* and the *CT log*
| `trusted-artifact-signer`
| Append-only logs. Rekor records every signature and attestation with the time it was made; the CT (certificate transparency) log records every certificate Fulcio issued. A signature cannot be made quietly, and its log time proves it was made while the certificate was valid.

| *TUF*
| `trusted-artifact-signer`
| Hands out the trust roots (Fulcio, Rekor, the CT log) that signers and verifiers start from.

| *Conforma*
| the pipeline's `verify-conforma` step
| Before deploy: Chains' signature and provenance verify (certificate identity and Rekor), the provenance says this pipeline's `build-image` built the image, and the image is pinned by digest. Any violation stops the run.

| *Policy controller*
| `policy-controller-operator`
| The admission webhook. Before any pod starts in a namespace labelled `policy.rhtas.com/include=true`, an image a `ClusterImagePolicy` names must carry Chains' signature: a Fulcio certificate with Chains' identity and its Rekor entry.
|===

Who signed this image?::
Tekton Chains, when `build-image` finished, as `spiffe://…/ns/openshift-pipelines/sa/tekton-chains-controller`.
The certificate names that identity and Rekor holds the record; the provenance names the pipeline run and task that built it.
How is it verified?::
With public material only: the Fulcio and Rekor roots from TUF, and the expected identity.
Conforma does it before deploy, the policy controller at admission, and you with `cosign verify` and `ec validate image`.
What does keyless mean?::
No stored private key. A key pair is made in memory for one signature and thrown away; trust comes from the identity in a 10-minute certificate and the log entry, not from guarding a secret.
Why does it still verify after the certificate expires?::
Rekor's entry time proves the signature was made inside the certificate's 10 minutes, so it stays verifiable long after.
That is what an auditor means by "prove it": not just a signature, but a receipt on a log they can read without contacting you.
Is the SBOM signed?::
Its sha256 is a result of the pipeline's `sbom` step, so it is part of the run's provenance that Chains signs.
Does it need the public Sigstore services?::
No. SPIRE, Fulcio, Rekor, the CT log and TUF all run in this cluster.

Chains can also sign with a stored cosign key, which needs no identity service; keyless trades guarding that key for an identity plane and a log, and gives every signature a public receipt.

== Policy as a gate, and policy as an admission control

There are two places to stop a bad image, and they are not interchangeable — a mature supply chain uses *both*.

Policy-as-gate (build time)::
A gate runs *inside the pipeline*: build the image, check it, and *fail the run* if it violates policy.
In this module's pipeline there are two: the RHACS scan (a known, blocked flaw) and *Conforma* (signed by Chains, built by this pipeline, pinned by digest).
Scanning gates in depth — a vulnerable dependency, a hard-coded secret, an insecure manifest — are the subject of the Application Security Testing module (a separate module).
The gate's value is *shift-left*: the problem is caught in the developer's own pipeline run, with a reason, before anything deploys.

Policy-as-admission (run time)::
A gate only helps if _every_ image goes through the pipeline.
*Admission* closes the gap by enforcing a rule at the cluster: the *Red Hat Trusted Artifact Signer policy controller* is an admission webhook that the API server asks about every pod, in every namespace that opts in with the label `policy.rhtas.com/include=true`.
Its `ClusterImagePolicy` (in `policy.sigstore.dev`) names the images it guards and the one signer it accepts: Tekton Chains' keyless identity, with a Rekor entry.
An image that never went through the pipeline is *refused before a pod exists*, whoever deploys it.
The gate is a policy you _run_; admission is a policy the platform _enforces_ whether or not anyone remembered to run the gate.
(OpenShift also has a node-level signature policy, `ClusterImagePolicy` in `config.openshift.io`, that the container runtime checks at pull time; the policy controller decides earlier, at the API, with a message the deployer reads.)
Note this is a *different* check from a scanner's deploy-time policy: the Application Security Testing module gates an insecure _manifest_ with `roxctl deployment check`, while admission here verifies the image's _signature_ — is it provably ours? — not its configuration.

.The scan in this lab is one gate

Your pipeline scans every image it builds with RHACS. The gate is scoped to the "Supply Chain Security" policies, so it fires *deterministically* on a known-bad dependency: the lab's optional challenge builds a branch carrying Log4Shell and watches the scan stop it before Conforma or the deploy run.

== Red Hat trusted content and the base image

The base image is the largest single source of what you ship, and it is the thread from the earlier build module.
`parasol-claims` builds `FROM` a Red Hat *Universal Base Image* (UBI) OpenJDK layer.
For a *trust* story, the point is *provenance*: UBI images are built and signed by Red Hat, so the bottom of your image already has a supply chain you did not have to assemble — a signed foundation you can verify rather than take on faith.
Keeping that base image *current* matters too, but for a different reason: an older tag carries known base-operating-system CVEs a newer tag has fixed.
That currency is a *scanning* concern (the Application Security Testing module), and the lab treats a base-image bump as exactly that — complementary hardening, a good habit, but *not* part of proving the image is yours.

== How this shows up in real orgs

* *When the next big CVE lands, having the receipts is what saves the weekend.*
Before SBOMs, "are we affected?" triggers a fire drill.
After, you have a stored, itemized record of what every image contains — the SBOM this pipeline attaches to each build.
_Querying_ those records at scale to find an affected component is the scanning story (the Application Security Testing module, and Red Hat Trusted Profile Analyzer); the *trust* story is that the record exists, is accurate, and travels with a signed image.
* *Signing is only half the story; enforcement is the other half.*
Plenty of organizations sign their images and then never check the signature at run time.
Admission control (the policy controller's `ClusterImagePolicy` here, or RHACS's cluster-wide admission) is what turns "we sign our images" into "the cluster will not run an unsigned one" — the difference between a policy on a wiki and a policy the platform enforces.
* *Provenance is what an auditor actually asks for.*
"Show me that the image in production came from this commit through this pipeline" is a real audit question.
A signed SLSA attestation is a machine-checkable answer — and because Tekton Chains produces it automatically, you get the answer without anyone writing it up. Conforma turns it into a rule the pipeline enforces.
* *Trust and scanning are complementary, not the same thing.*
A scanned-clean image that isn't signed can still be swapped for another; a signed image full of known CVEs is authentically vulnerable.
This module makes an image *provably yours*; the Application Security Testing module (a separate module) makes it *provably free of known flaws* — you want both, and they run in the same pipeline.

[NOTE]
.Where this sits — pipelines, scanning, and GitOps
====
The Pipelines Fundamentals module gave you a pipeline that produces an image and *data* (results).
This module adds the *trust* controls to that pipeline — a keyless signature and SLSA provenance on every image, an SBOM pinned by the signed provenance, a Conforma check before deploy, and admission that refuses anything the pipeline did not build.
Its sibling, *Application Security Testing* (a separate module), adds the *scanning* controls — SAST, SCA, DAST, and image-risk gates that prove the image is free of known flaws.
The GitOps modules that follow take the *trustworthy, signed* image and reconcile it into each environment — so admission control (only-signed-runs) and GitOps promotion are the two halves of "the right image, and only the right image, reaches production" (all separate modules).
====

== The trust layer over the Parasol platform

Everything you built in earlier modules — the namespace with its quota and RBAC, the app and its image build, the CI pipeline that produced it — has so far shipped on the strength of "the pipeline said it passed."
This module adds the layer that makes that provable: the scan and SBOM in the pipeline, Tekton Chains signing every image keyless and attesting its provenance in the Rekor transparency log, and Conforma and the admission webhook checking that signature before anything runs.
