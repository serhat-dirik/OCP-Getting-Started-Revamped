== What you built

You took Parasol's pipeline from "it builds" to "it is *trustworthy*", and every piece of evidence came from a run you started yourself.
One pipeline, `parasol-claims-trusted-build`, built `parasol-claims`, scanned it with RHACS, listed what is inside it in a CycloneDX *SBOM*, checked it with *Conforma*, and deployed it *pinned by digest*.
*Tekton Chains* signed the image and its *SLSA provenance* from outside the pipeline, *keyless*, as its own SPIFFE workload identity, with the record in the *Rekor* transparency log.
You then checked every claim with public material only: `cosign verify` against Chains' identity, the provenance naming your pipeline's `build-image`, the SBOM matching the hash in the signed provenance, and `ec validate image` repeating Conforma's verdict.
Last, you built the same source *outside* the pipeline and watched the admission webhook refuse it, while the pipeline's digest was admitted.

The idea under all of it: *trust is three facts about an artifact — what is inside it, who built it, and whether it is intact — produced automatically, recorded where anyone can audit them, and enforced by the platform*, so "prove this image is safe to run" is a query and a policy, not a promise.

== Map to your org

. *Do your builds produce and keep a bill of materials at all?*
You generated an SBOM and read what shipped in one line.
For a real service in your org: is there a stored, attestable record of what every image contains — one that travels with the signed image — or would "what's in this image?" mean a team grepping build files by hand?
(Querying those records across the estate when a CVE lands is the scanning module's job; this prompt is about whether the record _exists_ in the first place.)
. *Do you sign your images — and does anything check the signature?*
Signing without verification is theater.
If your org signs images today, where is the signature actually _enforced_ — at admission on every cluster, or nowhere?
If it does not sign yet, the Tekton Chains path you saw needs no pipeline step at all — what stops you turning it on?
. *Where should the gate live — the pipeline, admission, or both?*
You saw two build-time gates (the scan and Conforma) and a run-time admission control.
For a service that matters, which failures do you want caught in a developer's pipeline run (shift-left) versus refused at the cluster no matter what?
Most mature answers are "both" — which one is missing in your pipeline today?

== When *not* to reach for this

The controls in this module are the right default for anything you ship — which means being honest about their edges:

[cols="2,3",options="header"]
|===
| If you are about to… | Do this

| Switch on signature admission
| Scope it to the repositories you sign, keep a documented break-glass exception, and roll out warn-before-enforce

| Sign images that nothing verifies
| Turn signing on together with a place that enforces it

| Go keyless
| Run the identity plane (SPIRE) and the transparency log as production services first; a well-managed cosign key is simpler for an isolated build system

| Rely on admission alone
| Check in the pipeline too (Conforma), so a bad release fails with a reason before it deploys

| Treat an SBOM as a live feed
| Store SBOMs and re-query them when a new CVE lands
|===

.The reasoning behind each row
[%collapsible]
====
* *Don't switch on signature admission without scoping and a break-glass path.*
A signature policy that demands _your_ signature will also refuse *legitimate* images that were never signed with your key — a third-party base image, a vendor's operator, an emergency hotfix built off-pipeline.
Scope admission to the repositories you actually sign, decide who can grant a documented, time-boxed exception, and roll it out warn-before-enforce — otherwise the first unsigned-but-needed image turns a security control into an outage.
* *Don't sign images you never verify.*
If no admission control or downstream check ever validates the signature, signing is pure overhead.
Turn signing on _with_ a place that enforces it — otherwise you have added a step that produces an artifact nobody reads.
* *Keyless signing removes the key, not the operations.*
The keyless flow (Fulcio short-lived certificates + a Rekor transparency log) removes the long-lived key to guard and rotate, but every signature now depends on the identity plane (here SPIRE and its OpenID Connect (OIDC) discovery endpoint) and on the log, with its retention question.
Run them as production services before you depend on them; for an isolated build system, a well-managed cosign key can be the simpler choice.
* *Don't rely on admission alone.*
Admission refuses an image at the door, but the deployer learns about it only when a pod is refused.
A check in the pipeline (Conforma here) fails the release earlier, with a reason the developer reads in the run; admission stays the backstop for everything that skipped the pipeline.
* *An SBOM is a snapshot, not a subscription.*
The bill of materials is accurate the moment it is built; it does not tell you a component became vulnerable _after_ the build.
Storing SBOMs is what lets you re-query them when a _new_ CVE lands — the SBOM is only as useful as the process that keeps and re-scans it.
====

== Go deeper

* *Application Security Testing* — the *scanning* half of DevSecOps and this module's closest sibling: SAST, SCA, DAST, configuration, and image-risk gates that prove an image is *free of known flaws*, where this module proves it is *provably yours*. You want both, running in the same pipeline (a separate module).
* *GitOps Fundamentals* — this module made the image _trustworthy_ and taught the cluster to refuse an _unsigned_ one; the GitOps module is where that signed image is reconciled into each environment from Git, so "only the right image reaches production" is the two halves together (a separate module).
* *Registry, Images & Catalog Governance* — the other half of image admission: which registries images may come from at all, mirroring, and catalog governance (a separate module).
* *Red Hat Trusted Profile Analyzer* (RHTPA) — the enterprise "SBOM at scale" story: ingest every build's SBOM and query your whole estate for an affected component from one place (the RHADS component this lab's terminal `jq` foreshadows).
* Red Hat documentation: *OpenShift Pipelines* "Using Tekton Chains for supply chain security"; *Trusted Artifact Signer* (RHTAS), including its policy controller; *Conforma*; *Advanced Cluster Security for Kubernetes* (RHACS) build-time policy; and OpenShift "Managing secure signatures with sigstore" for the node-level `ClusterImagePolicy`.
