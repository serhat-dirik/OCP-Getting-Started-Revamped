== A safe sandbox for a team: grant, scope, enforce · 11 min

Two teammates and a workload share a namespace that governs none of them, and eleven minutes later each has exactly the access it should have — with the platform refusing root on its own.

[IMPORTANT]
.Prep — run before the session
====
This demo runs from the module's *entry* state: two teammate ServiceAccounts that nothing has granted access to, and a root-demanding workload scaled to zero. Nothing later on is staged in advance — every grant, and the rejection, happens live. Materialize the entry state a few minutes ahead:

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
ws prep multi-tenancy-workload-security
----

Every step runs in this cockpit's terminal, so give the terminal most of the screen and a large font. The two shell shortcuts the later steps use are set in step 1's first command.

CAUTION: `ws prep multi-tenancy-workload-security` is also the reset between runs. It returns the ServiceAccounts to ungoverned and the workload to zero replicas, and it removes the `edit` and `deployer` bindings and the `deployer` Role granted during an earlier run — so step 1 opens on a clean starting point every time.
====

=== Brief

image::multi-tenancy-workload-security/multi-tenancy-workload-security-01-identity-flow.svg["The chain from a login to a permission: an identity provider — htpasswd, Keycloak or LDAP — authenticates the user alice, who is a member of the payments-team group, which a RoleBinding ties to a Role or ClusterRole, which grants verbs such as get, list and create on resources such as pods, deployments and secrets."]

*Identity, then authority*

Every request to the cluster answers two questions in order. Authentication — who are you? — is settled by an identity provider wired into OpenShift's OAuth server: an htpasswd file, an LDAP directory, or an OpenID Connect provider such as Red Hat build of Keycloak. It proves you are `alice`, and says nothing about what you may do.

Authorization — what may you do? — is settled by RBAC, and the design lever is the third link in that chain: bind to the group, not to the person. A new engineer inherits every permission the group already holds, with no hunt through dozens of individual bindings. Workloads are governed the same way, as ServiceAccounts, which is why a team's CI robot is an identity you bind a role to rather than a shared password.

*Non-root by default*

A container on OpenShift cannot run as root just by asking. Each project carries a UID range, the `restricted-v2` Security Context Constraint draws every Pod's UID from it, and a Pod demanding `runAsUser: 0` is refused at admission — with nothing to install and no way for a namespace admin to grant themselves around it, because RBAC forbids granting a permission you do not hold yourself.

*In this demo*

. Two teammate ServiceAccounts and a workload sharing a namespace, with nothing granted to any of them
. A developer identity given full access in dev and read-only in prod, verified per environment
. A CI identity given a custom Role that ships deployments but cannot read the payment secrets
. A workload that demands root, refused by the platform at admission

=== 1 · Three service accounts, none of them governed — 2 min

Start where the payments team starts. Their dev namespace holds two teammate ServiceAccounts and one workload, and no role has been bound to any of them.

image::multi-tenancy-workload-security/multi-tenancy-workload-security-03-resourcequota-gauges.png["The workshop-quota ResourceQuota detail page in the team's dev project, showing six used-versus-hard donut gauges across the top: limits.cpu at 2.5% used, limits.memory at 1% used, persistentvolumeclaims at 0 of 10, pods at 1 of 30, requests.cpu at 1.7% used and requests.memory at 1% used, above the Name, Namespace and Labels details."]

*Why it matters*

Half of the sandbox is already in place before the team asks for anything: the namespace was born under a ResourceQuota that caps what it may consume, and that half is the platform team's to set, not the tenant's. The other half is missing entirely — nothing yet says *who*, or *what*, may act inside it.

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
NS={user}-dev; PROD={user}-prod
oc get sa payments-ci payments-ops root-demander -n $NS
----

`oc adm policy who-can` answers the question an auditor asks first — who may change anything here — and today it names nobody from this team:

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
if WHO=$(oc adm policy who-can update deployments -n $NS); then
  echo "$WHO" | grep -i payments || echo "payments teammates: UNGOVERNED (no access yet)"
else
  echo "step failed: who-can errored — check you are logged in and $NS exists"
fi
----

=== 2 · Full access in dev, read-only in prod — 3 min

Grant the first teammate — a developer identity — `edit` in the dev namespace and `view` in prod, then verify that one identity really does hold two different levels of access.

[mermaid]
....
include::example$diagrams/multi-tenancy-workload-security/05-access-per-namespace.mmd[]
....

*Why it matters*

Access is granted per namespace, not per person. A RoleBinding grants its role in one namespace only, so the same identity can be a full developer in dev and a reader in prod — which is what makes an environment promotion a governance decision rather than a second account to manage.

Grant the two roles:

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
NS={user}-dev; PROD={user}-prod
oc adm policy add-role-to-user edit -z payments-ci -n $NS
oc adm policy add-role-to-user view system:serviceaccount:$NS:payments-ci -n $PROD
----

Then verify the outcome rather than the intent:

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
echo "--- can-i (the proof) ---"
echo -n "dev  update deployments: "; oc auth can-i update deployments --as=system:serviceaccount:$NS:payments-ci -n $NS
echo -n "prod update deployments: "; oc auth can-i update deployments --as=system:serviceaccount:$NS:payments-ci -n $PROD
----

*What just happened*

`yes` in dev, `no` in prod, for one identity — and the difference between them is two RoleBindings, not two accounts.

=== 3 · A CI identity that ships but cannot read secrets — 3 min

The second teammate is the CI robot. `edit` would let it deploy — and also read every Secret in the namespace — so give it a custom Role instead: deployments and pod logs, and nothing else.

[mermaid]
....
include::example$diagrams/multi-tenancy-workload-security/06-deployer-boundary.mmd[]
....

*Why it matters*

Least privilege stops being a slogan once it is an object you can review. Two ServiceAccounts on the same team, one able to read the payment secrets and one blind to them, and the difference is a Role that fits in a pull request.

Author the Role:

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
NS={user}-dev; PROD={user}-prod
oc apply -n $NS -f - <<'EOF'
apiVersion: rbac.authorization.k8s.io/v1
kind: Role
metadata: {name: deployer}
rules:
  - {apiGroups: ["apps"], resources: ["deployments","deployments/scale"], verbs: ["get","list","watch","create","update","patch","delete"]}
  - {apiGroups: [""], resources: ["pods","pods/log"], verbs: ["get","list","watch"]}
EOF
----

Bind it to the CI ServiceAccount:

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
oc adm policy add-role-to-user deployer -z payments-ops --role-namespace=$NS -n $NS
----

Then check both halves of the boundary:

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
echo -n "deploy? "; oc auth can-i create deployments --as=system:serviceaccount:$NS:payments-ops -n $NS
echo -n "read secrets? "; oc auth can-i get secrets --as=system:serviceaccount:$NS:payments-ops -n $NS
----

*What just happened*

The CI identity ships deployments and is blind to Secrets. That is the difference between a CI token that is a convenience and one that is a breach.

=== 4 · The platform refuses root, live — 3 min

Scale the root-demanding workload to one replica. No Pod appears — the ReplicaSet's own event carries the admission decision, naming the constraint that refused it and the value it refused.

image::multi-tenancy-workload-security/multi-tenancy-workload-security-01-deploy-0of1-event.png["The root-demander ReplicaSet's Events tab, streaming one event from replicaset-controller and already repeated 16 times. The message is the whole lesson: Error creating: pods root-demander-... is forbidden: unable to validate against any security context constraint, followed by every SCC the platform tried and why each said no — provider restricted-v2 refusing .containers[0].runAsUser: Invalid value: 0: must be in the ranges 1001990000 to 1001999999, and every privileged provider simply Forbidden: not usable by user or serviceaccount."]

*Why it matters*

Nothing was installed to make this happen and nothing was configured: `restricted-v2` is the default constraint every workload on the cluster already runs under. A namespace admin cannot grant their way around it either — the built-in `admin` role has no rule for Security Context Constraints at all, which is exactly what makes the sandbox safe to hand to a team.

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
NS={user}-dev; PROD={user}-prod
----

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
oc scale deploy/root-demander --replicas=1 -n $NS
sleep 6
oc get deploy root-demander -n $NS
RS=$(oc get rs -n $NS -l app=root-demander --sort-by=.metadata.creationTimestamp -o jsonpath='{.items[-1:].metadata.name}')
MSG=$(oc get events -n $NS --field-selector involvedObject.name="${RS:-none}" -o jsonpath='{range .items[*]}{.message}{"\n"}{end}' \
  | grep -o 'provider restricted-v2: [^,]*, [0-9]*]' | tail -1)
echo "${MSG:-rejection event not found yet — wait a few seconds and re-run}"
----

*What just happened*

The Deployment reports `0/1`, and the rejection names its reason exactly: `restricted-v2: runAsUser: Invalid value: 0`. There are two honest resolutions. Fix the image so it stops demanding root — almost always the right one, and usually one line — or grant a scoped exception to that one ServiceAccount. Blanket-granting `anyuid` re-opens the door the platform just closed.

== Frequently asked questions

*If `admin` means admin, why can a namespace admin not apply a ResourceQuota or grant an SCC?*::
Because RBAC forbids privilege escalation: nobody can grant a permission they do not already hold. The built-in `admin` role deliberately omits write access to `resourcequotas` and `limitranges` and carries no SCC rule at all, so a namespace admin cannot lift their own quota or smuggle a workload to root. It means admin *of this namespace's contents and RBAC*, not admin of the cluster's guardrails — and that scoping is what makes the sandbox safe to hand to a team.

*This image runs as root on other clusters — why does OpenShift reject it?*::
The default `restricted-v2` constraint assigns every workload a non-root UID from the namespace's own range and refuses `runAsUser: 0`. It is a security default rather than a defect: most software does not need root, and the container only has to tolerate an arbitrary UID — a numeric non-root user and group-writable paths. Two fixes are legitimate: fix the image, which is almost always right, or grant a scoped exception to the one ServiceAccount that genuinely needs it (`oc adm policy add-scc-to-user nonroot-v2 -z <sa>`). Never blanket-grant `anyuid`.

*`who-can get secrets` returns over a hundred subjects — is the cluster misconfigured?*::
No, that is normal and healthy. Platform controllers, operators, the kubelet, and every namespace's own builder and deployer ServiceAccounts legitimately need Secret access somewhere; the count on a cluster like this one is around a hundred. That number is why the discipline is to always filter — `who-can … | grep <team>` for the subjects you actually govern. An audit that dumps a hundred rows is one nobody runs; a filtered `who-can` is a deliverable.

*Should each team get its own namespace, or its own cluster?*::
Default to namespace-per-team: RBAC, quota and NetworkPolicy isolate teams cheaply and densely on a shared cluster, and that is what everything shown here runs on. Step up only for a real boundary — hard regulatory separation, or a contractually capped blast radius — and even then, Hosted Control Planes (a per-tenant API server sharing worker capacity) is usually the right rung before a full cluster per team. Isolation is a cost multiplier; buy only what a specific requirement forces you to.

.The scoped exception, and why fixing the image wins
[%collapsible]
====
Workloads that genuinely need a fixed or elevated UID exist: a legacy database image, a node agent.
The answer is *not* to run everything as root; it is a *narrowly scoped* exception: a namespaced RoleBinding to a less-restrictive SCC (`nonroot-v2`, or `anyuid` only if UID 0 is truly required) targeting *one ServiceAccount*.
That exception is a platform-team grant: requested, reviewed, and scoped to one ServiceAccount.
The friction is deliberate, and it is why fixing the image almost always wins.

== 2. Give the workload a safe identity

The Pod you just fixed runs *as* the `root-demander` ServiceAccount, and nobody typed a password.
How does a workload prove who it is, without a secret that lives forever?

*You'll end with:* a 10-minute bound token for `payments-ci`, and its decoded claims.

List the secrets `payments-ci` has:

.Expected output (sample — the trailing suffix is random, generated fresh each time the ServiceAccount is created)
[source,texinfo]

*What just happened:* only a `dockercfg` pull secret, *no* `service-account-token`: current OpenShift no longer generates a permanent token Secret per ServiceAccount.

Mint a *bound token* on demand instead:

[source,sh,role=execute]
----

----

It is a JWT: *never print or paste it into chat*. Decode its claims (the middle segment):

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
oc create token payments-ci -n $NS --duration=10m \
  | cut -d. -f2 | base64 -d 2>/dev/null | tr ',' '\n' | grep -E '"exp"|"sub"|"namespace"' | head -3
----

The capture below is the sample user's `user1-dev`; yours reads `{user}-dev`.

.Expected output (`exp` is a Unix timestamp ~600 s from now)
[source,texinfo]
----
"exp":1783881069
"kubernetes.io":{"namespace":"user1-dev"
"sub":"system:serviceaccount:user1-dev:payments-ci"}
----

*What just happened:* `exp` is the 600 seconds you asked for, and `sub` names exactly one ServiceAccount in one namespace.

.Why this is workload identity done right
[%collapsible]
====
Workloads authenticate as ServiceAccounts the way people authenticate through an identity provider, and RBAC governs them identically.
A bound token gives a bounded lifetime, an audience of the cluster API, and a subject that names exactly which ServiceAccount in which namespace.
Short-lived, scoped, and — when mounted in a Pod — invalidated the moment the Pod is deleted.
It is the same least-privilege pattern an AI agent uses to reach the cluster in a later module.
====

=== Do it: put the guardrails on your own namespace

This block defines a `probe` helper and starts a small web server, `web`, in `{user}-dev`:

[source,sh,role=execute,subs="attributes+"]
----
NS={user}-dev; PROD={user}-prod
IMG=image-registry.openshift-image-registry.svc:5000/openshift/tools:latest
probe() {
  echo -n "from $1: "
  oc run probe -n "$1" --image="$IMG" --rm -i --restart=Never --quiet --command -- \
    sh -c "curl -s -m 3 -o /dev/null http://web.$NS.svc:8080 && echo connected || echo 'blocked (no answer in 3s)'"
}
oc run web -n $NS --image=$IMG --port=8080 --expose --command -- python3 -m http.server 8080
oc wait pod/web -n $NS --for=condition=Ready --timeout=60s
----

`probe` lives only in this terminal; if it reconnects, run this block again (`oc run web` then reports a harmless `AlreadyExists`).

*Predict, then run:* a Pod in `{user}-prod` calls `web` in `{user}-dev`. Connected, or blocked?

[source,sh,role=execute]
----
probe $PROD
----

.Expected output
[source,texinfo,subs="attributes+"]
----
from {user}-prod: connected
----

*What just happened:* connected. A namespace is not a network boundary by default: any Pod on the cluster could have made that call.

apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy

  name: deny-by-default
spec:
  podSelector: {}          # every Pod in the namespace
  policyTypes: [Ingress]   # ...and no ingress rule: nothing may connect
---

kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata:
  name: allow-from-same-namespace
spec:
  podSelector: {}
  ingress:
    - from:
        - podSelector: {}  # any Pod in THIS namespace
EOF

== Challenge (optional)
⏱ *no hand-holding*

Try these on your own:

* *Scope a Role too widely, then catch it:* swap `payments-ops` to the built-in `admin` role, catch the over-grant with `who-can`, then put it back.
* *Prove the escalation rule:* bind `payments-ci` to `cluster-admin` inside your own namespace, and read the refusal.
* *Mint a token and use it:* act *as* `payments-ci` with its bound token, and confirm the token is denied Secrets in `{user}-prod`.

.Hints
[%collapsible]
====
* *Scope a Role too widely, then catch it.* Grant `payments-ops` the built-in `admin` role in `{user}-dev` instead of `deployer`, then use `oc adm policy who-can get secrets -n {user}-dev | grep payments` to prove it can now read Secrets — the over-grant you'd flag in a review. Put it back to `deployer`.
* *Prove the escalation rule.* As your namespace `admin`, try to bind `payments-ci` to `cluster-admin` inside your own namespace (`oc create rolebinding payments-ci-cluster-admin --clusterrole=cluster-admin --serviceaccount={user}-dev:payments-ci -n {user}-dev --dry-run=server`). The refusal says you are `attempting to grant RBAC permissions not currently held` — you can't hand out a power you don't hold, even in a namespace you administer. (A *ClusterRoleBinding* would fail sooner, for a plainer reason: you cannot create cluster-scoped bindings at all.)
* *Mint a token and use it.* Create a bound token for `payments-ci` (`oc create token payments-ci -n {user}-dev`), then use it with `oc --token=… --server=… get pods -n {user}-dev` to act *as the ServiceAccount*, and confirm the same token is denied `get secrets` in `{user}-prod`.
====

[%collapsible]

`ws prep --yes` also removes the RoleBindings, Role and NetworkPolicies you authored by hand in this lab, so the teammates return to fully ungoverned and `{user}-dev` is open again.
