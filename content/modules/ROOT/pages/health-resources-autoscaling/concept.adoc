[.lead]
Monday, 9 a.m.: every Parasol claims adjuster signs in at once.
One claims pod is still booting and already taking requests, another has run out of memory, and nobody is awake to add a third.

This module teaches the platform three things about the claims service: when it can *serve*, how much it *needs*, and when to *add more of it*.
You add health probes, set requests and limits, read what happens when a limit is too small, and let a HorizontalPodAutoscaler (HPA) grow the service under load.

== What you'll learn

* What the platform *does* with each of the three health probes — *readiness*, *liveness* and *startup* — and why a readiness gate holds traffic away from a pod that cannot serve.
* Why a wrong liveness probe is worse than none.
* How *requests* and *limits* differ, which one the scheduler reads and which one the kernel enforces, and what *Quality of Service (QoS)* class a pod ends up in.
* What `OOMKilled` means, and how to read it from a pod's last termination state.
* How a *HorizontalPodAutoscaler (HPA)* adds replicas when CPU rises with traffic, and why it needs a CPU request to do it.

== Running is not the same as ready

A pod can be started and still loading; it can be alive and wedged.
The platform cannot tell those apart unless the application says so, and it says so through *probes*: small health checks the kubelet, the node agent that runs your containers, makes on a schedule.
There are three, and the platform does something *different* with each:

[cols="1,3,3",options="header"]
|===
| Probe | The question it answers | What the platform does on failure

| *startup* | "Has the app finished booting?" | Holds the other two probes off until this passes, so a slow start is not mistaken for a failure.
| *readiness* | "Can the app take traffic *right now*?" | Removes the pod from the Service's endpoints: traffic is *held*, not sent to a pod that would fail it.
| *liveness* | "Is the app still working, or wedged?" | *Restarts* the container.
|===

The claims service exposes these as HTTP endpoints — `/q/health/started`, `/q/health/ready` and `/q/health/live` — which its Quarkus runtime provides out of the box.
The one that matters most day to day is *readiness*: a pod that answers no stops receiving traffic until it answers yes again.

Readiness also guards every rollout.
A new pod that never becomes ready never replaces the old one, so a bad change is *held back* while the old version keeps serving.

Liveness is the probe to be careful with.
Point it at the wrong path, or at something the app does not control, and the platform will dutifully restart a healthy container over and over.
That is why liveness should ask "is *this process* healthy?", never "is the database up?".

== Requests, limits, and what the kernel enforces

Every container can declare two numbers for CPU and for memory:

*Request*:: What the scheduler *reserves* for the container, and what it uses to decide which node has room for the pod.
The HPA also measures CPU use as a percentage *of the request*.
*Limit*:: The ceiling the container may not cross.
Over the CPU limit, the container is *throttled*: slower, but still running.
Over the memory limit, the kernel *kills* it, and the pod reports `OOMKilled`.

....

Your namespace already fills in defaults through a *LimitRange* (here a request of `100m` CPU and `256Mi` memory, and a limit of `1500m` and `1Gi`), so a container that declares nothing still gets numbers.
Defaults are a safety net, not a sizing decision: in the lab you replace them with values chosen for the claims service.

The two numbers also decide a pod's *Quality of Service (QoS)* class, which is the order the node evicts pods in when it runs short of memory:

* *Guaranteed* — every container's requests equal its limits; evicted last.
* *Burstable* — requests are set but lower than limits (the common case, and the claims app's).
* *BestEffort* — no requests or limits at all; evicted first.
The LimitRange means you will not see this one in your namespace.

=== When the limit is too small: OOMKilled

A memory limit is enforced by the Linux kernel, not by the application.
When the container crosses it, the process is killed on the spot — no exception, no stack trace, no graceful shutdown.
The pod's last termination state reads `reason: OOMKilled` with exit code `137`, and the container restarts into the same wall until the limit changes.

A Java application needs memory beyond its heap — class metadata, compiled code, threads — before it serves a single request.
Set the limit below that and it never finishes starting.
In the lab you do exactly that, read the evidence, and put the limit back.

== Scaling is a decision, not a reflex

"It's slow — add replicas" is only sometimes the right answer.
OpenShift gives you four scaling tools, and picking the wrong one wastes money or does nothing.

....

* *HorizontalPodAutoscaler (HPA)* — more replicas when CPU or memory rises with traffic.
This is the workhorse, and what you build in the lab.
It needs a resource *request* on the container to compute utilization against.
* *Serverless (Knative)* — scale to *zero* when idle and back up on the first request.
Right for bursty or rarely used services; see the xref:serverless-zero-to-hero/concept.adoc[Serverless Zero-to-Hero] module.
* *KEDA (Custom Metrics Autoscaler)* — scale on an external signal such as queue depth, not CPU.
Right for batch and event-driven work; see xref:jobs-batch-kueue/concept.adoc[Jobs, Batch & Queued Workloads] and xref:eventing-deep-dive/concept.adoc[Eventing Deep-Dive & Serverless Workflows].
* *VerticalPodAutoscaler (VPA)* — right-*size* one pod's requests and limits instead of adding pods.
Useful, but never point a VPA and an HPA at the same resource on the same workload: they fight.
VPA is concept-only here.

The HPA's loop is simple: sample CPU, compare the average with the target, and change the replica count to close the gap.
What takes time is the *sample*, not the decision — which is why, in the lab, the scale-out takes a minute or two rather than a few seconds.

== How this shows up in real orgs

* *Fewer 2 a.m. pages from half-started pods.*
A readiness probe turns "the deployment went out and users saw errors for a minute" into "the deployment waited until the new pods could serve".
* *Right-sizing is a cost conversation.*
Requests are reserved capacity, used or not.
Teams that copy `requests: 4 CPU` from a template pay for idle cores across every replica; teams that measure and set requests honestly fit more on the same nodes.
* *`OOMKilled` is the most common "it just restarts" ticket.*
Knowing to read the last termination state — not the current logs, which show nothing — saves hours.
* *Capacity follows demand.*
An HPA lets a service run lean overnight and grow for the Monday peak, without anyone filing a ticket to scale it.
Keeping enough of those pods running through node maintenance is the job of a PodDisruptionBudget, covered in xref:deployment-targets-scheduling/concept.adoc[Deployment Targets & Scheduling].
