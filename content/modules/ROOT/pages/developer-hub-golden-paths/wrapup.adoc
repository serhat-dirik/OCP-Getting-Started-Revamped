== What you built

You stepped into a new Parasol engineer's first day and took a service from nothing to production without assembling any of the plumbing yourself.
One form in Red Hat Developer Hub, *Parasol service, build to production*, created two repositories in your team's organization: `parasol-quote-{user}` with the code, its devfile and its `.tekton/` triggers, and `parasol-quote-{user}-gitops` with its pipelines and its dev, stage and prod configuration.
The platform team's wiring for your team picked them up on its own: Argo CD Applications for `ci`, `dev`, `stage` and `prod` appeared, and the app repository was connected to Pipelines-as-Code.

You changed the code in Dev Spaces and pushed.
The build pipeline ran the unit tests, built the image, checked it with Red Hat Advanced Cluster Security, recorded an SBOM, waited for Tekton Chains to sign it keyless, and verified it with Conforma, and only then wrote its digest into the dev overlay for Argo CD to deploy.
Then you tagged a release, and the release pipeline promoted that *same* digest to stage and on to prod.
A tag pushed before its build had passed was refused, and your second release met a stable version in prod, so Argo Rollouts ran it as a canary with an automated analysis.
You watched every step from the component's *CI*, *CD* and *Topology* tabs.

== Map to your org

. *How long does it take your organization to get a new service to production with every practice in place?*
You did it with one form, a few pushes and two tags.
Think about your last new service: was it created from a template the platform team owns, or copied from an existing repository and then fixed up in review?
How many tickets, people and days sat between "we need a service" and "it is signed, scanned and promoted through Git"?
. *Which of your standards are enforced by construction, and which by audit?*
Pick one you care about: an SBOM per image, signature verification before deploy, canary releases, deploy by digest.
Do new services *have* it because the template gave it to them, or do you *find it missing later* and file a ticket?
. *Who in your organization would own the golden template, and how would they know it is used?*
A template is a product with users.
Who changes it when a policy changes, who decides whether a practice lives in each service's files or in a shared definition, and what would tell you that teams are routing around it?

== When *not* to reach for this

A golden template is powerful, and like every capability in this workshop it earns its place only sometimes.

[cols="3,4",options="header"]
|===
|If… |Then…

|You have a handful of services and one team
|Skip the portal and the template for now: a short shared repository of examples is cheaper to keep current

|Nobody will own the template after it ships
|Do not ship it: an unmaintained template stamps out last year's practices into every new service

|Services differ more than they match (runtimes, release cadence, compliance regime)
|Write a small set of templates for the common cases; leave the rest hand-built

|The template is the *only* allowed way
|Leave room to drive off the road with a reason, or teams will route around it

|A practice must change everywhere at once (a new mandatory gate, a policy fix)
|Keep that practice in a shared definition every service references, not in generated files
|===

.The reasoning behind each row
[%collapsible]
====
* *A template is upkeep, not a one-off.*
Its value is that it carries the platform team's current best answer.
Without an owner it carries an old answer, faithfully, into every service created from it.
* *Don't let templates sprawl.*
One paved road is leverage; twenty subtly different templates move the copy-and-drift problem up a level.
Keep the set small and opinionated, and retire the ones nobody chooses.
* *Don't turn the paved road into a cage.*
The generated project is plain files in the team's repositories so a team can adapt it.
If a team cannot leave the road for a legitimate need, they leave the platform instead.
* *Generated files versus shared definitions: the trade-off this template makes.*
A template generates files *once*.
Services created last year keep last year's pipeline unless someone updates them.
A shared pipeline that every service references by name changes everywhere with one commit, but every team gets the same pipeline, and a mistake reaches everyone at once.
This template splits the difference: the *tasks* (clone, build, scan, verify) come from the shared task library, so a fix to a task reaches every service; the *pipeline* (which tasks, in what order) lives in each service's GitOps repo, so a team can add a gate without asking.
When a practice must be identical everywhere, such as the release policy, move it into a shared definition.
* *The portal is production software.*
Red Hat Developer Hub is an application you deploy, upgrade, secure and back up, with plugins to keep current.
That is a fair cost when it removes cognitive load for a whole engineering organization, and a poor trade for a few services that were never confusing.
====

== Go deeper

* *Pipelines Fundamentals & Task Libraries*, *Application Security Testing (SAST · SCA · DAST)*, *Trusted Software Supply Chain*, *GitOps Fundamentals* and *GitOps at Scale & Progressive Delivery*: each practice in the template, taught on its own (separate modules).
* *Dev Spaces & the Inner Loop*: the generated `devfile.yaml` and the workspace it defines (a separate module).
* *Authoring your own golden path*: a Software Template is a form (`spec.parameters`) plus steps (`spec.steps`: `fetch:template`, `publish:gitea`, `catalog:register`), with the skeleton files it renders living beside it in Git.
Improving the skeleton once improves every future service, the platform team's highest-leverage edit.
* *More gates*: the build pipeline is the place to add the gates this template leaves out to stay fast, such as static analysis and dynamic testing; the tools that fill them are your organization's choice.
* *Developer Lightspeed for Red Hat Developer Hub*: an AI assistant that answers questions about your services and the platform from inside the portal (distinct from *OpenShift Lightspeed*, the console assistant).
* Red Hat documentation: the *Red Hat Developer Hub* guides (software catalog, dynamic plugins, the Kubernetes, Topology, Tekton and Argo CD plugins, and authoring Software Templates), and the *OpenShift Pipelines* and *OpenShift GitOps* guides.
Read the version matching the release your cluster runs.

.Clean up
====
Leave everything running if you are continuing; nothing depends on tearing it down.
When you want a clean entry state, run `ws prep {module-id} --yes`: it empties your `{user}-svcs` organization (both repositories), removes the service from the catalog, deletes its Argo CD Applications, and purges `{user}-dev`, `{user}-stage`, `{user}-prod` and `{user}-cicd`.
Your seeded Parasol forks in your personal Git namespace are never touched.
